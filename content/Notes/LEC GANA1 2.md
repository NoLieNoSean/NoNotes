# Basic structures

Let $\Omega$ denote a general set. Symbols denoted by script letters such at $\mathcal{A}$ represent classes of subsets of $\Omega$. 

> [!Definition] Algebra
> A family $\mathcal{A}$ is said to be an **algebra** if
> 1. $\Omega\in \mathcal{A}$;
> 2. $E\in \mathcal{A}\implies E^{c}\in \mathcal{A}$; 
> 3. $E, F\in A\implies E\cup F\in \mathcal{A}$. 

$\sigma$-algebras are algebras that are closed under countable unions:

> [!Definition] $\sigma$-algebra
> A family $\mathcal{B}$ is called a **$\sigma$-algebra** if 
> 1. $\Omega\in \mathcal{B}$; 
> 2. $E\in \mathcal{B}\implies E^{c}\in \mathcal{B}$; 
> 3. If $\{ E_{n} \}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq \mathcal{B}$, then $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in \mathcal{B}$. 

Being closed under complements and countable unions implies being closed under countable intersections.

> [!Proposition]
> If $\mathcal{S}$ is any collection of subsets of $\Omega$, then there exists a smallest $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$ which contains $\mathcal{S}$, given by 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \bigcap \{ \mathcal{B}:\mathcal{B}\text{ is a }\sigma\text{-algebra and }\mathcal{S}\subseteq \mathcal{B}  \}.
> \end{align}
> $$
> We call this minimal $\sigma$-algebra the **$\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{S}$** and denote it by $\sigma(\mathcal{S})$. 

^bfc1ae


A monotone class is a family closed under monotone limits:

> [!Definition] Monotone class
> A family $\mathcal{M}$ is said to be a **monotone class** provided:
> 1. If $E_{1}\subseteq E_{2}\subseteq\dots$ and $E_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$ $\forall n$, then $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$; and
> 2. If $E_{1}\supseteq E_{2}\supseteq\dots$ and $E_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$ $\forall n$, then $\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$. 

Given a family $\mathcal{S}$, the **monotone class generated by $\mathcal{S}$** is defined analogously to [[#^bfc1ae]] and denoted by $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})$. 

> [!Proposition] Monotone class lemma, @athreyaMeasureProbability2018 Prp 1.3.2
> If $\mathcal{A}$ is an algebra of subsets of $\Omega$, then $\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})=\sigma(\mathcal{A})$.

# Measures

> [!Definition]
> Let $\mathcal{A}\subseteq P(\Omega)$ be an algebra, and $\mu:\mathcal{A}\to[0, \infty]$. 
> 1. $\mu$ is said to be **finitely additive** if $A, B\in \mathcal{A}$, $A\cap B=\emptyset$ $\implies$ $\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B)$. 
> 2. $\mu$ is said to be **countably additive** or **$\sigma$-additive** if it satisfies the following criterion: If $\{ A_{n} \}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is a countable collection of sets in $\mathcal{A}$ such that (i) $A_{n}\cap A_{m}=\emptyset$ for $n\ne m$, and (ii) $A=\coprod_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in \mathcal{A}$, then $\mu(A)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n})$. 
> 3. $\mu$ is said to be **countably subadditive** if it satisfies the following criterion: If $\{ A_{n} \}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is a countable collection of sets in $\mathcal{A}$ such that $A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in \mathcal{A}$, then $\mu(A)\leqslant \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{n})$.
> 4. $\mu$ is said to be **monotone continuous** if $A_{n}\uparrow A\in \mathcal{A}\implies \mu(A_{n})\uparrow \mu(A)$. 

> [!Definition] Measure
> 1. $\mu$ is called a **measure** if $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and $\mu$ is  $\sigma$-additive. 
> 2. A measure $\mu$ is said to be **finite** if $\mu(\Omega)< \infty$. 
> 3. A measure $\mu$ is said to be **$\sigma$-finite** if there exist sets $\{ A_{n} \}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq \mathcal{A}$ with $\mu(A_{n})< \infty$ for all $n\in \mathbb{N}$ which satisfy $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}=\Omega$. 

> [!Proposition]
> Suppose $\mu$ is finitely additive. Then, $\mu$ is $\sigma$-additive iff $\mu$ is monotone continuous. Thus, measures are monotone continuous. 
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > $(\implies)$ Suppose $A_{n}\uparrow A\in \mathcal{A}$. Define $B_{n}=A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1}$, $A_{0}=\emptyset$. The $B_{i}$ are disjoint, so $\mu(A)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(B_{n})=\lim_{ n \to \infty }\mu(A_{n})$. 
> > 
> > $(\impliedby)$ Suppose $\{ A_{n} \}$ is a countable collection of pairwise disjoint sets with their union in $\mathcal{A}$. Define $B_{n}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}$. Then, $B_{n}\uparrow A$. By monotone continuity, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n})=\lim_{ n \to \infty }\mu(B_{n})=\mu(A)$.
>

> [!Proposition]
> A measure is countably subadditive. 
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > Let $\mu$ be a measure on $\mathcal{A}$. Let $\{ A_{n} \}$ be a countable collection of sets from $\mathcal{A}$ whose union $A$ lies in $\mathcal{A}$. From finite additivity and induction, for each $n\in \mathbb{N}$ we have
> > $$
> > \mu(A_{1}\cup\dots \cup A_{n})\leqslant \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mu(A_{k}) \leqslant \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{k}).
> > $$
> > Since $\mu$ is monotone continuous, $\mu\left( \bigcup_{k=1}^{n}A_{k} \right)\uparrow \mu(A)$. Thus, we must have
> > $$
> > \mu(A)\leqslant \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{k}).
> > $$
> > 
>

> [!Proposition]
> Let $\mu:\mathcal{A}\to[0, \infty]$ with $\mu(\emptyset)=0$. Then, $\mu$ is a measure on $\mathcal{A}$ iff $\mu$ is finitely additive and countably subadditive. 
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > $(\implies)$ follows from [[#^267c6a]]. 
> > 
> > $(\impliedby)$ Suppose $\{ A_{n} \}\subseteq \mathcal{A}$ are pairwise disjoint with union $A$ in $\mathcal{A}$. By subadditivity, we have
> > $$
> > \mu\left( A \right)\leqslant \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{n}).
> > $$
> > We can obtain the reverse inequality by using finite additivity:
> > $$
> > \sum_{n=1}^{k} \mu(A_{n})=\mu\left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{k} A_{n} \right)\leqslant \mu(A)
> > $$
> > holds for all $k$, so $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n})\leqslant \mu(A)$. 
>

> [!Proposition] @athreyaMeasureProbability2018 1.4.6 (2)
> Let $\mu$ be a measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$. Suppose $\{ A_{n} \}\subseteq \mathcal{F}$ with $A_{n}\downarrow A$ and $\mu(A_{1})< \infty$. Then $\mu(A_{n})\downarrow \mu(A)$.
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > \mu(A_{1}) & =\mu\left( A_{1}\cap\left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_{n}^{c} \right) \right)+\mu(A) \\
> >  & =\mu\left( \coprod_{n=1}^{\infty}(A_{n}\setminus A_{n-1})  \right)+\mu(A) \\
> >  & =\left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_{n})-\mu(A_{n-1}) \right)+\mu(A) \\
> >  & =\mu(A_{1})-\lim_{ n \to \infty } \mu(A_{n})+\mu(A).
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> > Since $\mu(A_{1})\ne \infty$, we have $\mu(A_{n})\downarrow \mu(A)$. 
> 

^943813

As an example of why we need $\mu(A_{1})< \infty$, consider the sets $\{ n, n+1, \dots\}\downarrow \emptyset$ in $\mathbb{N}$ endowed with the counting measure. 

> [!Definition] limsup and liminf
> Let $\mu$ be a measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$. Let $\{ A_{n} \}\subseteq \mathcal{F}$. Define
> $$
> \limsup A_{n}:=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigcup_{m=n}^{\infty} A_{m},
> $$
> $$
> \liminf A_{n}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigcap_{m=n}^{\infty} A_{m}.
> $$
> 

Clearly, $\lim\inf A_{n}\subseteq \limsup A_{n}$. Recall [[LimSup and LimInf#^6a1a47|analogous definitions for sequences]]. 

> [!Proposition]
> Let $\mu$ be a *finite* measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$, and $\{ A_{n} \}\subseteq \mathcal{F}$. Then
> $$
> \mu(\liminf A_{n})\leqslant \liminf\mu(A_{n})\leqslant \limsup\mu(A_{n})\leqslant \mu(\limsup(A_{n})).
> $$
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > We will prove the rightmost inequality; in the middle is the standard inequality for sequences ([[LimSup and LimInf#^596c29]]). 
> > 
> > Define $B_{n}=\bigcup_{m=n}^{\infty}A_{n}$. They form a monotone decreasing sequence. By [[#^943813]] (this is where we use the finiteness hypothesis), 
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > \mu(\limsup A_{n})=\mu\left( \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} B_{n} \right)=\inf \mu(B_{n}).
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> > Now, 
> > $$
> > \begin{align}
> > \mu(B_{n}) & \geqslant  \mu(A_{n}) & \forall m\geqslant  n \\
> >  \implies \mu(B_{n}) & \geqslant  \sup_{m\geqslant  n}\mu(A_{n}) \\
> > \implies \inf_{n} \mu(B_{n}) & \geqslant  \inf _{n}\sup_{m\geqslant  n}\mu(A_{n}) \\
> > \implies \mu(\limsup A_{n}) & \geqslant   \limsup\mu(A_{n}).
> > \end{align}
> > $$
> > 
> > The leftmost inequality is proved analogously to the rightmost one. Additionally, it doesn't require the finiteness hypothesis, since we invoke the fact measures are monotone continuous instead of [[#^943813]]. 


> [!Proposition] Borel-Cantelli
> Let $\mu$ be a *finite* measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$. Suppose $\{ A_{n} \}\subseteq \mathcal{F}$ such that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_{n})< \infty$. Then $\mu(\limsup(A_{n}))=0$. 
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > As before, let $B_{n}=\bigcup_{m=n}^{\infty}A_{m}$. Using the summability hypothesis, $\mu(B_{n})\leqslant \sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\mu(A_{m})\to 0$. Using [[#^943813]], $\mu(\limsup A_{n})=\inf \mu(B_{n})=0$. 
>

# The Caratheodory extension

> [!Lemma]
> Let $\mathcal{F}\subseteq P(\Omega)$ be a $\sigma$-algebra. Let $\Omega_{0}\subseteq \Omega$, and $\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{ A\cap \Omega_{0}:A\in \mathcal{F} \}$. Then $\mathcal{F}_{0}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra. Further, If $\mathcal{F}=\sigma(S)$ for some $S\subseteq P(\Omega)$, then $\mathcal{F}_{0}=\sigma(S_{0})$, where $S_{0}=\{ A\cap \Omega_{0}:A\in S \}$. 
> 
> > [!Proof]-
> > 
> > That $\mathcal{F}_{0}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra is clear. 
> > 
> > Let $\mathcal{F}=\sigma(S)$ for some $S\subseteq P(\Omega)$, and $S_{0}$ be as defined above. Since $S_{0}\subseteq \mathcal{F}_{0}$, we have $\sigma(S_{0})\subseteq \mathcal{F}_{0}$. It remains to show the reverse inclusion. Define
> > $$
> > \Sigma=\{ A\in P(\Omega):A\cap \Omega_{0}\in\sigma(S_{0}) \}.
> > $$
> > Again, it is clear that $\Sigma$ is a $\sigma$-algebra. Since $S\subseteq\Sigma$, we have $\sigma(S)\subseteq\Sigma$, or $\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Sigma$. Intersecting each element of $\mathcal{F}$ and $\Sigma$ with $\Omega$, we obtain $\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\sigma(S_{0})$. 
> 

^1a58ae

> [!Proposition] Caratheodory extension
> Suppose $\mu$ is a measure on an algebra $\mathcal{A}$. Then there exists a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{M}\supseteq \mathcal{A}$ and a measure $\tilde{\mu}$ on $\mathcal{M}$ such that $\tilde{\mu}|_{\mathcal{A}}=\mu$. Further, if $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite, then the extension is unique on $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$. 

^9aa087


We will explicitly construct the extension in the next lecture; for now, we will prove the uniqueness claim. 

> [!Proof]-
> 
> Let $\mu_{1}$, $\mu_{2}$ be measures on $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$ such that $\mu_{1}|_{\mathcal{A}}=\mu_{2}|_{\mathcal{A}}=\mu$ .
> 
> To begin, assume $\mu$ is finite; this forces finiteness of $\mu_{1}$ and $\mu_{2}$. Let $\mathcal{M}=\{ A\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}):\mu_{1}(A)=\mu_{2}(A) \}$. It suffices to prove $\sigma(\mathcal{A})\subseteq \mathcal{M}$. Since we already have $\mathcal{A}\subseteq \mathcal{M}$, and the monotone class generated by $\mathcal{A}$ is equal to $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$ by [[#^9a3a4b]], it suffices to prove $\mathcal{M}$ is a monotone class. 
> 
> Suppose $A_{n}\uparrow A$, $A_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$. By [[#^f6c633]], $\mu_{1}(A_{n})\uparrow \mu_{1}(A)$ and $\mu_{2}(A_{n})\uparrow \mu_{2}(A)$. Since $\mu_{1}(A_{n})=\mu_{2}(A_{n})$ for all $n$, it follows that $\mu_{1}(A)=\mu_{2}(A)$. Similarly, if $A_{n}\downarrow A$ for $A_{n}\in \mathcal{M}$, we can use [[#^943813]] (we assumed $\mu$ is finite) to conclude $\mu_{1}(A)=\mu_{2}(A)$. Thus, $\mathcal{M}$ is a monotone class. 
> 
> Now let $\mu$ be $\sigma$-finite, so that $\Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{n}$, $\Omega_{n}\in \mathcal{A}$, $\Omega_{n}\subseteq \Omega_{n+1}$, $\mu(\Omega_{n})< \infty$. Recall [[#^1a58ae]]. Define $\mu_{i}^{n}$ on $\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{ A\cap \Omega_{n}:A\in\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \}$ by $\mu_{i}^{n}=\mu_{i}|_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}$ for $i=1, 2$. Define $\mu^{n}$ on $\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{ A\cap \Omega_{n}:A\in \mathcal{A} \}$ by restricting $\mu$. 
> 
> Since each $\mu^{n}$ is a finite measure on $\mathcal{A}_{n}$, and $\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{n})$, $\mu^{n}_{1}=\mu^{n}_{2}$ on $\mathcal{F}_{n}$. For $\mathcal{A}\in\sigma(\mathcal{A})$, 
> $$
> \begin{align}
> \mu_{1}(A) & =\sup\mu_{1}(A\cap \Omega_{n}) \\
>  & =\sup\mu^{n}_{1}(A\cap \Omega_{n}) \\
>  & =\sup\mu_{2}^{n}(A\cap \Omega_{n}) \\
>  & =\mu_{2}(A).
> \end{align}
> $$
>
