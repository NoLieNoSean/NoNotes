# Preliminaries

Given a field $k$ and a positive integer $n$, we define the $n$-dimensional **affine space** over $k$ to be the set 
$$
k^{n}=\{ (a_{1}, \dots, a_{n})\ | \  a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}\in k \}.
$$
> [!Proposition] @coxIdealsVarietiesAlgorithms2015 1.5
> Let $k$ be an infinite field and let $f\in k[x_{1}, .., x_{n}]$. Then $f=0$ iff the evaluation map $f:k^{n}\to k$ is the zero function.

It follows that two polynomials in a polynomial ring over an infinite field are equal iff they have the same evaluation map.

We say that a field $k$ is algebraically closed if every nonconstant polynomial in $k[x]$ has a root in $k$.

> [!Definition]
> Let $k$ be a field, and let $f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}$ be polynomials in $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$. Define
> $$
> \mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}):=\{ \mathbf{a}\in k^{n}\ | \  f_{i}(\mathbf{a})=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq s \}.
> $$
> $\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s})$ is called the **affine variety** defined by $f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}$.

For example, the solutions to a system of linear equations with coefficients in $k$ is a *linear variety* ($\mathbf{b}+\ker A$). By the rank nullity theorem, the dimension of a linear variety is determined by the number of independent equations. This idea applies to general varieties with an appropriate generalization of 'independence'.

> [!Proposition]
> If $V=\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}), W=\mathbf{V}(g_{1}, \dots, g_{t})\subseteq k^{n}$ are affine varieties, then so are $V\cup W$ and $V\cap W$, and are given by
> $$
> \begin{align}
> V\cap W & =\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}, g_{1}, \dots, g_{t}) \\
> V\cup W & =\mathbf{V}(f_{i}g_{j}\ | \  1\leq i\leq s, 1\leq j\leq t).
> \end{align}
> $$

A Variety depends only on the ideal generated by its defining equations; affine varieties are determined by *ideals*, not equations. 

> [!Definition]
> Let $V\subseteq k^{n}$ be an affine variety. Define
> $$
> \mathbf{I}(V)=\{ f\in k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]\ | \  f(\mathbf{a})=0\text{ for all }\mathbf{a}\in V \}.
> 
> $$
> It is easy to see that $\mathbf{I}(V)$ is an ideal.

We now have the following maps
$$
\begin{array}{cccccc}
\text{polynomials} &  & \text{variety} &  & \text{ideal} \\
f_{1}, \dots, f_{s} & \to & \mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}) & \to  & \mathbf{I}(\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s})).
\end{array}
$$
It is always true that $\langle f_{1}, \dots, f_{s} \rangle\subseteq \mathbf{I}(\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}))$. Equality may not occur: $\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{V}(x^{2}, y^{2}))=\langle x, y \rangle$, and $\langle x^{2}, y^{2} \rangle\subset \langle x, y \rangle$. For algebraically closed fields, there is a straightforward relation between these ideals given by the Nullstellensatz. 

Ideals in $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ and affine varieties in $k^{n}$ are in a bijective correspondence:

> [!Proposition]
> Let $V$ and $W$ be affine varieties in $k^{n}$. Then, 
> 1. $V\subseteq W$ iff $\mathbf{I}(V)\supseteq \mathbf{I}(W)$.
> 2. $V=W$ iff $\mathbf{I}(V)=\mathbf{I}(W)$.

Important questions about ideals in $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$:
1. Is every ideal finitely generated? *Yes, by Hilbert's basis theorem*
2. Is there an algorithm to decide if $f\in k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$ lies in $\langle f_{1}, \dots, f_{s} \rangle$ for $f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}\in k[x_{1}, .., x_{n}]$? *Yes, Buchberger's algorithm*
3. What is the relation between $\langle f_{1}, \dots, f_{s} \rangle$ and $\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{V}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}))$? *Hilbert's Nullstellensatz*

## A simple model: polynomials in one variable

Polynomial rings in one variable over fields are nice (see @coxIdealsVarietiesAlgorithms2015 [p. 53]):

1. They are [[LEC ALG3 9#Euclidean domains|euclidean domains]], so gcd is defined and the euclidean algorithm can be used to find it;
2. They are PIDs, so checking membership of a polynomial $f$ in an ideal $\langle f_{1}, \dots, f_{s} \rangle=\langle \text{gcd}(f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}) \rangle$ reduces to checking divisibility of $f$ by the gcd of $f_{1}, \dots, f_{s}$.

Gröbner bases allow us to generalize the division algorithm to $k[x_{1}, \dots, x_{n}]$.
