What does it mean for religion and state to be treated as separate entities? This is a hard question to answer. A much easier question to tackle is "What would happen if religion and state are NOT separate?".

Can a religion be a state? No. Here's why.

Religion, in its characteristic myopic lack of vision, tries to concretely codify human morality. Human morality is not fixed, and it changes with time, independent of religion as our knowledge and science develops. A few centuries years ago, exploiting slaves was morally acceptable, and so was male dominance. A few decades ago, homosexuality was considered moral decay. There certainly are things that we routinely do today without facing any moral objections that will be considered vile in the future, or the other way around. To effectively govern a society, a government's policy must reflect the morals that society holds. This is exactly why religion is intrinsically incapable of stable, long term governance - the beliefs and tenants of a religion are immutable, since they are considered to be the decree of God himself. Religion has a very strong inertia against change for this reason. Additionally, since the government derives its power from the purported truth of its beliefs, the challenge of religion is an existential threat to the state. Thus, if becomes quintessential for the continued existence of the government to ensure religious submission of its people and to stop the morality of the society from evolving (which goes hand in hand with scientific progress). The overturning of Roe v Wade, the suppression of women in Afghanistan, The Catholic Church discouraging the use of and spreading misinformation about condoms and the resulting impact on the HIV/AIDS epidemic in Africa are all examples of religion forcing its policies based on a bygone standard of morality on people. 

Now, you might ask, that's a fair point, but can't the massive inertia created by religion also stop the morality of a society from plummeting? No. The phenomenon of theocratic governments strictly enforcing their religion on their citizens, but themselves acting in ways that brazenly defy their religious tenets is quite frequent and has a long history. The contradiction between the strict enforcement of religious law on citizens and the blatant disregard for those principles by governing elites is quite striking. Here are a few examples:

**Iran (Post-1979):** After the 1979 Islamic Revolution, Iran became an Islamic theocracy under Ayatollah Khomeini, with Sharia law enforced strictly on citizens. However, there have been numerous documented instances of hypocrisy among Iran’s ruling elite. While ordinary citizens face strict dress codes and prohibitions on Western culture, elite officials are often reported to enjoy luxury goods, Western education for their children, and even alcohol consumption — all of which are publicly condemned by the state.

**Saudi Arabia:** As a monarchy governed by Wahhabi Islam, Saudi Arabia enforces one of the strictest interpretations of Sharia law, imposing punishments for “moral” crimes such as premarital sex, alcohol consumption, and LGBTQ+ relationships. However, members of the royal family have often been reported to live lavish lifestyles abroad, engaging in activities strictly forbidden by their laws, including drinking and gambling.

**The Papal States (Middle Ages to 1870):** The Catholic Church’s control over central Italy through the Papal States is another historical example. While enforcing strict moral and religious laws on the populace, church leaders, including several Popes, were known to engage in practices that violated their own teachings, such as corruption, lavish spending, and even nepotism. Pope Alexander VI, for instance, is notorious for fathering multiple children despite the vow of celibacy and for amassing wealth through indulgences.

## refined


**Motion**: Religion and state should be *treated* as separate entities.

**What is a State? What is a Religion?**

A _state_ is an organized political community, governing society through laws, policies, and regulations, designed to adapt to the changing needs and values of its people. _Religion_, on the other hand, is a system of beliefs centered around moral or spiritual principles, often tied to unchanging doctrines considered to be divinely inspired or absolute.

**What does it mean for religion and state to be separate?**

Rather than answering this directly, let’s examine the consequences if religion and state were _not_ separate. What would happen if religion wielded political power or if government decisions were subject to religious doctrines?

**Can a Religion Be a State?**

Religion often tries to codify human morality in absolute terms, rooted in beliefs considered immutable. Religion has a very strong inertia against change for this reason. However, human morality isn’t fixed—it evolves over time as knowledge, science, and social values advance. Centuries ago, morally acceptable practices included ownership and trade of people and the immolation of widows on the funeral pyres of their husbands; only decades ago, homosexuality was considered moral decay. There certainly are things that we routinely do today without facing any moral objections that will be considered vile in the future, and the other way around. The state, therefore, must be adaptive to align with the evolving morals of its people to govern effectively.

This is exactly why religion is intrinsically incapable of stable, long term governance - the beliefs and tenants of a religion are immutable, since they are considered to be the decree of God themself. A government tied to a fixed doctrine cannot adapt to new knowledge or values without facing a crisis of legitimacy as effectively as a government that is not. Because its authority rests on religious dogma, challenges to its tenets are an existential threat to such a government. To maintain control,  it becomes absolutely essential for the government to enforce strict religious adherence among its citizens and prevent societal evolution in morality, which incidentally often parallels scientific and philosophical progress.

The suppression of women’s rights in Afghanistan, the overturning of Roe v. Wade, and the Catholic Church’s historical dissemination of misinformation on condom use (with severe consequences during the HIV/AIDS crisis in Africa) illustrate the harm caused when religious dogma dictates policy.

**Can Religion Stabilize Society Morally?**

Some of you may argue that religion offers a moral anchor, preventing societal “moral decay” -  that the same inertia that adamantly resists the evolution of human morality for the better can prevent it from plummeting. But it doesn't. The phenomenon of theocratic governments strictly enforcing their religion on their citizens, but themselves acting in ways that brazenly defy their religious tenets is quite frequent and has a long history.

Consider a few examples:

• **Iran (Post-1979):** Following the Islamic Revolution, Iran became a theocracy under Sharia law, enforcing strict rules on citizens. Yet Iran’s ruling elite have been documented enjoying luxuries and freedoms (such as Western education for their children and even alcohol consumption) that they publicly condemn.

• **Saudi Arabia:** Saudi Arabia imposes severe punishments for moral crimes, including premarital sex and alcohol consumption. Yet members of the royal family are often reported to enjoy lavish lifestyles abroad, frequently engaging in activities forbidden by their own religious laws.

• **The Papal States (Middle Ages to 1870):** The Catholic Church once governed central Italy with strict moral laws. But church leaders, including popes, often engaged in practices (such as nepotism and lavish spending) that violated their teachings. Pope Alexander VI is infamous for fathering multiple children despite a vow of celibacy.

These examples underscore how theocratic governments often hold citizens to religious standards while the ruling class disregards those same principles, revealing the hypocrisy inherent in such systems.

**In Conclusion**

To sum up: a theocratic state results in a stifled, suppressed society that is not allowed to progress its philosophy or science lest the people start having, *nudge, nudge*, "ideas", with the upper echelon putting up a facade of religious compliance for the masses while getting to privately flout them in the most grandiose manner then can conceive. To be clear, we are not claiming that a secular democracy prevents such activity. Our main argument is that a theocracy dampens human development. Thus, at this point I think we are comfortable in making the statement that a society should at the very minimum attempt to separate religion and state. This might not be ever achievable in the complete sense. But the very act of attempting to separate the two spheres requires society to "treat" or distinguish between religion and state as separate entities. By consistently working to keep religion and state as separate as possible, we allow society to evolve freely, advancing in science, human rights, and individual freedoms without the burden of outdated dogmas. This separation is not just desirable—it is essential for a truly free and progressive society. 


## Rebuttals!

*But religious beliefs are not immutable! While the scriptures may remain unchanged, the medium though which society interfaces with the scriptures, such as priests, change with time, and with them, the interpretation of scripture. From time to time, there are also religious reformers like Raja Ram Mohan Roy.*

It’s true that religious interpretation can adapt over time, and reformers can push for change. However, this flexibility is limited by the underlying scripture itself. For certain issues, such as abortion, interpretations can vary based on different understandings of when life begins, allowing for debates framed within religious values. Yet, on other topics, particularly those where scripture is explicit, reinterpretation is far more constrained.

Take, for instance, the issue of homosexuality. The Bible addresses this with striking clarity:

> “If a man lies with a man as with a woman, both of them have committed an abomination; they shall surely be put to death; their blood is upon them.”
> 
> Leviticus, Chapter 20, verse 13

While there are indeed Christians who interpret this or choose to disregard such passages, the literal wording presents a significant barrier to reinterpretation without straying from traditional religious tenets. I acknowledge that people are often comfortable holding logically inconsistent beliefs, and there may be interpretations that soften or bypass the condemnation of homosexuality. However, these interpretations often come at the cost of confronting the explicit text and facing accusations of deviating from “true” doctrine.

What I’m willing to assert is this: while non-religious societies are not devoid of prejudices, they are generally more flexible in redefining social norms because they are not beholden to fixed religious decrees. Thus, while a Christian society can evolve in its views, the rate and extent of change are often slower due to the tether to scriptural authority. You do not get to appeal to god for free. The price is flexibility.

**How is a religious text different from a secular constitution? They wield supreme authority in theocracies and secular democracies respectively. They are both highly resistant to change.**

The thing to bear in mind is that the life cycle of a state and of a religion play out on different scales. States form and dissolve over centuries, but religions stick around for millennia. Thus, the constitution of a secular state is much more likely to be better aligned with the morality and values of its society than a religious work.
