- Is a map $S\times S\to S$.

# Subgroups of the additive group of integers

- Subgroups of the additive group $\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ are are of the form $\mathbb{Z}a$, where $a\in \mathbb{Z}$.

# Cyclic groups

The cyclic subgroup $H$ generated by $x$ is the set of all elements that are powers of $x$. If $H$ is finite, the set of integers $k$ such that $x^{k}=1$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^{+}$. The order of an element $x$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $x^{n}=1$. 

The Klein-4 group is the simplest non-cyclic group.

# Homomorphisms

A homomorphism is a structure preserving map $h$ between two groups $G$ and $G'$, i.e, $h(ab)=h(a)h(b)$. Homomorphisms map identity to identity, and inverses to inverses. 

The kernel and image of a homomorphism are subgroups.

Definition of left cosets.

Let $\psi:G\to G'$ be a homomorphism of groups, and let $a$ and $b$ be elements of $G$. Let $K$ be the kernel of $\psi$. Then, the following are equivalent:
- $\psi(a)=\psi(b)$
- $a^{-1}b$ is in $K$
- $b$ is in the coset $aK$
- The cosets $bK$ and $aK$ are equal.

The last point can be proved by showing that $aK$ and $bK$ are subsets of each other.

A subgroup $N$ of a group $G$ is a *normal subgroup* if for every $a\in N$ and every $g\in G$, the conjugate $gag^{-1}$ is in $N$. In other words, $N=gNg^{-1}$. 

The kernel of a homomorphism is a normal subgroup.

# Isomorphisms

An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism.

If $\phi:G\to G'$ is an isomorphism, so is $\phi ^{-1}:G'\to G$. Note that $\phi ^{-1}$ is well defined because $\phi$ is a bijection.

So, for computation with the group law, the two groups have identical properties.

Every group of order $p$ where $p$ is prime is cyclic.

An isomorphism $\phi:G\to G$ is called an automorphism. A *conjugation map* is an automorphism: Let $g$ be a fixed element of $G$. Conjugation by $g$ is the map $\phi:G\to G$ defined by $\phi(x)=gxg^{-1}$ (Easy to verify that this is a homomorphism, and is bijective since conjugation by $g^{-1}$ is a inverse). Two elements $x, x'\in G$ are said to be conjugates if $x=gx'g^{-1}$ for some $g\in G$. Conjugate elements have the same algebraic properties, like order, since they are linked by an isomorphism.

The *commutator* of $a, b\in G$ is defined to be $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. $a$ and $b$ commute, i.e, $ab=ba$, iff the commutator of $a$ and $b$ is $1_{G}$. 
# Equivalence relations and partitions

Conjugacy is an equivalence relation.

Any MAP OF SETS $f:S\to T$ gives an equivalence relation on its domain $S$, defined by $a\sim b$ is $f(a)=f(b)$. The inverse images of the elements in the image of $f$ are the equivalence classes induced by the relation.

If the map is a group homomorphism $\phi:G\to G'$, the equivalence class of any $a\in G$ is the coset $aK$, where $K=\ker \phi$. As always, this partitions $G$ into equivalence classes, which in this case are left cosets of $K$. 
# Cosets

The cosets of $H$ in $G$ are the equivalence classes for the congruence relation $a\equiv b$ id $b=ah$ for some $h\in H$. 

Thus, the left cosets of a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ partition the group.

All left cosets of $H$ in $G$ have the same order, since the map $h\mapsto ah$ is bijective, with $h\mapsto a^{-1}h$ being the inverse.

The number of left cosets of $H$ in $G$ is called the index of $H$ in $G$, denoted $[G:H]$. May be infinite when $G$ is infinitie.

Since all cosets have the same order, and since they partition the group, we have the Counting formula, $|G|=|H||[G:H]|$. 

Lagrange's Theorem

Let $\phi:G\to G'$ be a homomorphism of finite groups. Then, 
- $|G|=|\ker\phi|\cdot |\mathrm{Im}\phi|$
- $|\ker\phi|$ divides $|G|$
- $|\mathrm{Im} \phi|$ divides both $|G|$ and $|G'|$.

Multiplicative property of the index: $[G:K]=[G:H][H:K]$.

### Right cosets

Right cosets $Ha$ are the equivalence classes for right congruence, i.e, $a\equiv b$ if $b=ha$ , $h\in H$. Right cosets also partition $G$, but the partition induced by the right cosets and the left cosets may be different. They are the same for normal subgroups, though.

Equivalent statements:
- $H$ is a normal subgroup
- $gHg^{-1}=H$ for all $g\in G$
- the left coset $gH$ is equal to the right coset $Hg$ for all $g\in G$.
- Every left coset of $H$ is a right coset.


Proposition
- If $H<G$, $g\in G$, then $gHg^{-1}<G$. 
  Proof: $gHg^{-1}$ is the image of $H$ under an automorphism (isomorphism). 
- If $H<G$ is the only subgroup of $G$ of order $r$, then $H\triangleleft G$.
  Proof: The conjugation of $H$ by $g$, $gHg^{-1}$, is a group of order $r$.
